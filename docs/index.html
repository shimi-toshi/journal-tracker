Unintended Regulatory Spillovers: The Impact of Bank Regulatory Oversight on Environmental Enforcement

John Gallemore, Vincent Giese
2026/10/13 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70088
Abstract

We explore whether bank regulatory oversight affects the ability of environmental enforcement to reduce industrial emissions. A major objective of environmental enforcement is to reduce industrial emissions by encouraging firms to employ greener production processes and technologies. However, such changes often require external financing obtained from banks, and prior research shows that regulatory oversight of banks can affect bank lending. We find that, on average, the reduction in air pollution in response to county‐level environmental enforcement is attenuated in counties exposed to increased bank regulatory oversight, as measured by severe enforcement actions. Furthermore, we show that the negative impact of bank regulatory oversight on small business lending to affected counties is the likely mechanism behind this on‐average effect. However, when bank regulatory oversight targets banks with stronger capitalization, we find that the negative effect of bank regulatory oversight on environmental enforcement is mitigated. Collectively, our findings suggest that bank regulation has a nuanced effect on the extent to which environmental enforcement reduces emissions, indicating that one regulator's mission can impact the objectives of another.

The effect of the mandatory disclosure of corporate tax returns on reporting bias

ERIC J. ALLEN, HENRY L. FRIEDMAN, AYDIN UYSAL
2026/10/11 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70087
Abstract

How do investor reactions to the mandatory disclosure of firm tax information influence managers' reporting choices? To answer this question, we build a structural model of reporting bias, which we test with a unique data set of Turkish firms that have been mandated to publicly disclose their tax returns since 2009. We find that both book and tax income numbers are informative to investors. Structural estimates are consistent with disclosure of the tax report reducing both market incentives to manage book earnings and investor uncertainty about underlying firm value. Tax disclosure is associated with higher reported tax income, though the increase is not statistically significant. Counterfactual estimates suggest that book‐tax‐conformity could change financial and tax reporting biases, but lead to a lower quality information environment relative to the separate disclosure of book and tax reports.

Mimicking Regulatory Peers

MINJAE KIM
2026/09/30 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70086
Abstract

Bank regulators use peer information for bank evaluations and publicly disclose this information. This study investigates whether the regulatory use and disclosure of peer information induce herding behavior in banks' regulatory capital ratios. I examine this question using a 2004 peer group reform that introduced class‐of peer groups for newly chartered banks, grouping them exclusively with their cohorts, while established banks continued to be compared with similar‐sized banks. The results show that, post‐reform, banks exhibit heightened herding behavior in their regulatory capital ratios. Depending on their relative capital position, banks either become more sensitive to changes in the peer group average or converge toward it. Additionally, I find that under‐capitalized banks adjust loan portfolios to manage their capital ratios, and this gap‐closing behavior is associated with worse subsequent loan quality, higher bank failure rates during the financial crisis, and, at the bank holding company level, larger systemic‐risk contributions. These findings highlight significant implications of regulatory disclosure for bank behavior and stability.

Book Value Risk Management of Banks: Limited Hedging, HTM Accounting, and Rising Interest Rates

João Granja, Erica Xuewei Jiang, Gregor Matvos, Tomasz Piskorski, Amit Seru
2026/09/29 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70090
Abstract

We document that as interest rates rose in 2022, banks largely left long‐duration assets exposed to interest rate risk while shielding the accounting value of their balance sheets. Call report and Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) data show that only about 6% of U.S. banking assets were hedged with derivatives, and even the heaviest users hedged only a small share of their portfolios. Rather than hedge market‐value risk, banks relied on held‐to‐maturity (HTM) accounting to shield book capital, reclassifying about $700 billion of securities as HTM during the tightening period, more than twice the amount reclassified beforehand. Cross‐sectionally, banks with lower capitalization, more fragile uninsured funding, and greater long‐duration asset exposure were less likely to expand hedging during tightening and more likely to rely on HTM accounting. More vulnerable banks, especially those overseen by less stringent state regulators, were also more likely to shift into HTM. We develop a stylized model showing how higher interest rate risk, through its effects on bank solvency, run risk, and regulatory capital, shapes banks’ incentives to hedge, recapitalize, or rely on HTM accounting. While HTM accounting can help well‐capitalized banks avoid excessively tight capital constraints, it can also weaken hedging incentives among weaker, moderately capitalized banks by allowing them to window‐dress capital ratios while remaining exposed to runs. Our evidence suggests that this latter channel dominated during the tightening episode. Incorporating deposit franchise value into regulatory capital without accounting for run risk may further weaken the effectiveness of capital regulation. These findings carry important implications for regulatory capital accounting and bank risk‐management practices.

Partisanship, Information, and Punishment for Misconduct at Work

VIVEK PANDEY, JOANNA S. WU, YUANZHE ZHANG
2026/09/28 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70089
Abstract

We examine whether firms apply partisan standards when responding to employee financial misconduct. Using detailed individual‐level data on financial advisers, we find that advisers who are in the political minority at their firm (political minority advisers) are no different from their colleagues at the same firm and time in the incidence and severity of misconduct, yet following misconduct, they are significantly more likely to depart—a pattern we term “partisan punishment standards.” This pattern is especially pronounced at firms with lower internal information quality (IIQ), proxied with (1) lower external reporting quality (for public firms only), (2) private firms (relative to public firms), and (3) not hiring an independent public accountant to create an internal control report (for both public and private firms). We also document “partisan reporting standards”: Firms are more likely to publicly disclose misconduct by political minority advisers while remaining relatively silent in other cases. Firms that exhibit partisan punishment practices subsequently experience slower growth.

When Is It Fairer to Allocate Resources for Training to Lower Performing Versus Higher Performing Employees?

ERIC W. CHAN, MACKENZIE K. FEINBERG, MARTIN WIERNSPERGER
2026/09/20 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70085
Abstract

Professional development and training are essential for organizational growth and success, but resource constraints often force managers into a dilemma: Should they train their lower performing or higher performing employees? While lower performers with greater skill gaps can benefit more from training than higher performers, managers must also consider employees’ fairness concerns. Using an interactive experiment, we predict and find that how employees react to their manager's allocation of training resources depends on the prevailing fairness norms in the work environment. When egalitarian norms dominate, such as when employees have little control over their job tasks, lower performers take the receipt of resources for training for granted and react negatively when the manager allocates those resources to higher performers instead. In contrast, when meritocratic norms dominate, such as when employees have more control over their job tasks, higher performers react more negatively than lower performers to not receiving training resources. Additional experiments suggest that these fairness‐norm–dependent reactions are most pronounced under tournament incentives and may disappear under individual performance‐based pay. Overall, our study highlights the importance for managers to consider both employee productivity and the prevailing fairness norms of the work environment when allocating scarce training resources.

Do Commercial Property Taxes Affect Business Microlocation Decisions?

Brayden D. Bulloch, Fabio B. Gaertner, Jeffrey L. Hoopes
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2025-0248
Abstract

Do commercial property taxes affect business microlocation decisions? Using precise geolocations for 2.8 million U.S. establishments, we employ a spatial discontinuity density design, comparing for-profit establishments to nonprofits, which are generally exempt from property taxes. Relative to nonprofit establishments, for-profit establishments disproportionately locate on the low-tax side of county borders. This effect strengthens with the size of the tax differential. At borders with differentials in the top decile (quartile), we estimate a 6–7 (5–6) percent shift in establishment location toward the low-tax side over a 10-km band. Businesses move from as far as 10 km away on the high-tax side but concentrate within 1 km on the low-tax side. Firms trade off tax savings with customer access: low-foot-traffic establishments respond more to tax differentials than high-foot-traffic ones. A survey of U.S. mayors finds that perceptions of property taxes’ importance for business location rise with cross-border tax differentials. Data Availability: Survey data are available upon request. Unwinding Loan Prospecting Pressure through Renegotiation: The Role of Loan Officer Changes in Debt Contracting

Jessie Cheong, Jung Koo Kang, Regina Wittenberg-Moerman
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2025-0120
Abstract

We examine the impact of loan officer changes on debt contract design. Loan officers face pressure to originate new loans, incentivizing them to offer loan terms that are more favorable than warranted by borrowers’ creditworthiness. Because a loan officer who replaces the originating loan officer is not subject to this loan prospecting pressure, we predict that she reverses the favorable discretion exercised at loan origination. Consistent with this prediction, we find that the first renegotiation following the replacement of the originating loan officer is associated with more unfavorable renegotiation outcomes. This effect is more pronounced when loan prospecting pressure at loan origination is greater—when the originating loan officer is less experienced, when the lead arranger faces tight performance benchmarks or lower income growth, and when credit market competition is more intense. Overall, we provide novel insights into how loan officer changes restore contracting efficiency through renegotiations. Tax-Related Key Audit Matters and Changes in Multinational Income Shifting

Christof Beuselinck, Nathan C. Goldman, Jochen Pierk, Cinthia Valle Ruiz
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0168
Abstract

Key audit matters (KAMs) increase the salience of uncertainty surrounding the underlying accounts. We examine KAMs related to uncertain tax positions (UTPs), which highlight potential concerns to external stakeholders about uncertain tax planning activities such as multinational income shifting. Increased scrutiny may reduce the expected benefits of these strategies and discourage firms from pursuing them. Using KAM disclosures in the expanded audit reports, we find that group-level KAM disclosures are associated with a decline in multinational income shifting when the KAMs relate to UTPs. This relation is stronger when firms are financially unconstrained, when the institutional environment features strong tax enforcement, and when KAMs highlight specific risk statements and auditors provide explicit assurance. Overall, our findings reveal a novel and potentially unintended real effect of expanded audit reports: the salience of tax-related KAM disclosures influences multinational tax planning, highlighting broader consequences of this regulatory intervention. The Effect of Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income Tax on U.S. Multinational Companies’ Cross-Border Tangible Investments

Jingjing Huang, Benjamin Osswald, Ryan J. Wilson
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0158
Abstract

The Global Intangible Low-Taxed Income (GILTI) provision, enacted under the 2017 U.S. tax reform, aimed to deter profit shifting by U.S. multinational corporations (MNCs) but has raised concerns about unintended incentives to shift tangible investments abroad. Using an ex ante identification strategy based on public financial statements, we document that an average MNC increases foreign tangible investment by approximately $256.2 million relative to 2017 levels to minimize GILTI tax. Although this finding could raise concerns that firms are reducing domestic tangible investment for foreign expansion, we find no significant decline in domestic tangible investment. Furthermore, we find GILTI firms increase both foreign and domestic innovation, including capital-intensive technologies. From Text to Beta: Macroeconomic Disclosures and Firm-Level Systematic Risk

Cameron Holstead, Alon Kalay, Gil Sadka, Federico Siano
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0833
Abstract

Using news text from The Wall Street Journal and macroeconomic disclosures in 10-K filings, we address the challenge of characterizing both time-varying aggregate risks and firm-level risk exposures. We develop a disclosure-based measure of systematic risk that (1) reveals salient macro risks in each period, (2) provides a text-based estimate of earnings exposure to these risks, and (3) explains the cross-section of stock returns. We also show that macro disclosures are pervasive across the annual report, grow over time, vary with the business cycle, and exhibit within-firm variation, consistent with the time-varying nature of systematic risk exposure. Social Media Toxicity and Capital Markets

Elizabeth Blankespoor, Jedson Pinto, Kirti Sinha
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0017
Abstract

This paper asks about the existence, determinants, and implications of toxic content in financial social media. Using machine learning algorithms to measure toxicity in Seeking Alpha articles and comments, we find measurable toxicity concentrated primarily in user-generated comments rather than articles. Comment toxicity is greater for firms with more investor attention or uncertainty. We document three key results about toxicity in capital markets. First, toxicity displays a feedback loop in platform participation: past toxicity is associated with more future toxic contributors for a given firm. Second, firms receiving more toxic comments have greater retail trading volume but less informative retail trades, especially when comments disagree. Third, toxicity is associated with slower price discovery around earnings announcements. Our findings suggest that financial social media toxicity relates to both user behavior and market outcomes and raise important considerations for platform governance in financial markets. Building Better Information: Reporting Technology, Internal Information Frictions, and Investment Efficiency

Philip G. Berger, Feng Li, Lisa Yao Liu, M. H. Franco Wong
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2023-0595
Abstract

We assess how firms use spending on reporting infrastructure to improve managerial reporting quality (MRQ) and whether doing so improves investment efficiency. Using the Sarbanes-Oxley Act’s internal control provisions (SOX 404) as a shock to MRQ demand, we find that treated firms increase reporting infrastructure spending, including for information technology and communications. We next examine variation across segments in the benefits and costs of improving reporting infrastructure. Segments increase spending more when facing larger internal moral hazard frictions and when they are less likely to have voluntarily improved MRQ before SOX (including segments insulated from competition, facing difficulty obtaining enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems, or lacking internal control personnel). Finally, we find that treated firms improve investment efficiency post-SOX 404, with larger gains among firms with higher spending on reporting infrastructure. Our findings indicate that regulatory mandates can improve investment efficiency by inducing reporting infrastructure improvements that enhance MRQ. Determinants and Consequences of Auditors’ Consultations with the National Office

Matthew G. Sherwood, Miguel Minutti-Meza, Aleksandra B. Zimmerman
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0348
Abstract

Audit teams may consult with their firm’s national office to obtain specialized expertise, satisfy regulatory and quality control requirements, and demonstrate due professional care. We describe the consultation process based on PCAOB standards, firm methodologies, and auditor interviews. We explain different types of consultations and highlight differences in the determinants and usefulness of mandatory versus voluntary consultations. Using PCAOB inspection data, we show that formal consultations are largely predictable based on engagement characteristics and, on average, are not associated with audit quality. However, two subsets of formal consultations that focus on accounting issues and occur earlier in the engagement process are associated with greater regulatory compliance and higher audit quality. Hence, an increasing reliance on all types of consultations does not automatically translate into higher audit quality. Collectively, our findings deepen our understanding of auditors’ use of national office consultations and their implications for audit practice. Beyond Greenwashing: Environmental Commitments and Gas Flaring in the African Oil Sector

Samuel Chang, Hans B. Christensen, Andrew McKinley
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2025-0361
Abstract

Corporations make substantial progress toward their Zero Routine Flaring commitments globally, particularly in Africa where preexisting environmental standards are weak. Moreover, committers’ flaring reductions on the continent are driven primarily by operational improvements rather than offloading assets and are concentrated among committers facing greater stakeholder pressure. Although flaring is higher for assets continuously operated by, awarded to, or divested to noncommitters, corporate actions to uphold commitments are associated with a conservatively estimated 10 percent net reduction in flaring in Africa without a corresponding reduction in oil production or local economic activity in extraction communities. Internal Control over Financial Reporting and Mortgage Fraud

Abstract

Mortgage fraud was a leading force behind the 2008 financial crisis. We measure the fraudulent income overstatement at the bank-ZIP Code-year level as the difference in the local growth between reported income in mortgage applications and the income based on IRS data. We find that overstated income is positively associated with banks’ internal control weaknesses over financial reporting and negatively associated with subsequent remediation of such weaknesses. How Black Accounting Faculty Exercise Agency to Navigate Career Advancement within Institutional Structures

Michelle K. Harding, Jennifer R. Joe, Kerri-Ann Sanderson
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0836
Abstract

This study examines how tenured BAF experience and navigate institutional structures while advancing their careers. Leveraging a qualitative approach, we find BAF encounter recurring forms of marginalization embedded in organizational structures that lead to social identity threats that destabilize their identities as scholars and teachers. BAF respond to these threats by employing agentic and self-preserving coping responses designed to affirm identity and negotiate career advancement. Productivity versus Efficiency: The Effect of Incentive Focus on Target Setting in Participative Budgets

Jake Andrassy, Jason L. Brown, Timothy Mallon, Ashley K. Sauciuc
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2025-0493
Abstract

Firms typically design incentive systems to motivate employees and advance firm objectives, including productivity (i.e., maximizing output) and efficiency (i.e., minimizing input). However, there remains little empirical research on the potential psychological effects of adopting a productivity versus efficiency focus on employee risk perceptions and decision-making. To address this important gap, we examine how these different incentive foci affect employee target setting under participative budgets. We predict and find that employees perceive efficiency incentives as riskier than productivity incentives, leading to lower target setting. Moreover, consistent with our theory, this effect disappears in the absence of outcome uncertainty. We also find employees are less likely to achieve their target and have higher error rates under efficiency incentives, despite setting easier targets. Overall, our findings suggest that efficiency incentives, relative to productivity incentives, can have detrimental effects on target setting and subsequent performance in participative budgeting settings. Does Free Speech Law Contribute to Corporate Social Responsibility Behavior? Empirical Evidence

Paul A. Griffin, Hyun Hong, Boochun Jung, S. Katie Moon, Jiwoo Ryou
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2023-0659
Abstract

People’s right to free speech can have profound effects on how firms discharge their social and environmental responsibilities. We examine these effects by analyzing the impact of the staggered enactment of anti-SLAPP free speech laws across U.S. states on firms’ corporate social responsibility (CSR) behavior. We theorize that, following the enactment of anti-SLAPP laws, firms enhance their CSR activities as an economically effective means to counter negative publicity from stakeholders. Auditor-Employed Tax Experts and Audit Quality

Andrew M. Bauer, Miguel Minutti-Meza, Saad Siddiqui, Aleksandra B. Zimmerman
2026/09/01 | 10.2308/tar-2023-0495
Abstract

Using PCAOB data, we examine variation in auditor-employed tax experts’ involvement to assist the audit team. Tax expert hours average 5 percent of total audit hours and are positively associated with several proxies for the complexity of tax estimates. Greater tax expert involvement correlates with a higher detection rate of tax-related internal control material weaknesses (ICMWs) and a greater frequency of subsequent tax-related restatements. However, earlier tax expert involvement is associated with fewer ICMWs, although it is not significantly associated with subsequent restatements. Overall, our findings provide new evidence on how the timing and intensity of tax expert involvement relate to audit outcomes. Although involving tax experts is a typical response to auditing complex estimates, their involvement alone does not fully mitigate restatement risk. Importantly, timely coordination between the audit team and tax experts plays a critical role in ensuring high audit quality. Abstract

Purpose Fragmentation remains a central challenge in sustainability reporting, especially in the shift from voluntary to mandatory regimes. This study aims to examine whether Integrated Reporting (IIRF) quality aligns with sector-specific Sustainability Accounting Standards Board (SASB) disclosure quality under Chile’s dual mandate (NCG 461). Although both aim to enhance transparency, they reflect distinct logics, holistic value creation versus financially material, industry-specific disclosure. Whether these logics translate into aligned practice under a mandatory setting remains unclear. Design/methodology/approach Using 290 firm-year observations (2022–2023), this study constructs formative, framework-anchored disclosure quality indices for IR and SASB based on item-level coding. By documenting parallel disclosure under a mandatory regime, this study shows that formal convergence of standards does not automatically deliver decision-useful information for society, regulators or capital markets. Originality/value To the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study provides one of the first direct cross-framework tests of IR–SASB alignment under a mandatory dual-adoption regime.
