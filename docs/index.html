The Anti‐SLAPP Statutes and Corporate Environmental Policies

KEVIN C.W. CHEN, ZHIHONG CHEN, CHENGZHU SUN
2026/11/04 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70095
Abstract

We examine whether legal protection for civic participation affects corporate pollution. Polluters may deter public discipline through strategic lawsuits. Anti‐SLAPP (Strategic Lawsuit Against Public Participation) statutes mitigate this threat by enabling prompt dismissal and recovery of legal costs. Using a difference‐in‐differences design, we find that enacting an anti‐SLAPP statute in a state significantly reduces toxic emissions from that state by increasing local public pressure. The reduction is greater when the statutes provide stronger protection, when local stakeholders have stronger incentives and abilities to discipline polluters, and when local public pressure increases more after the enactment. Plants lower emissions by improving abatement rather than by reducing output or relocating emissions. Our paper underscores the role of legal protection in environmental governance and informs policy debates over anti‐SLAPP legislation.

Unintended Regulatory Spillovers: The Impact of Bank Regulatory Oversight on Environmental Enforcement

John Gallemore, Vincent Giese
2026/10/13 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70088
Abstract

We explore whether bank regulatory oversight affects the ability of environmental enforcement to reduce industrial emissions. A major objective of environmental enforcement is to reduce industrial emissions by encouraging firms to employ greener production processes and technologies. However, such changes often require external financing obtained from banks, and prior research shows that regulatory oversight of banks can affect bank lending. We find that, on average, the reduction in air pollution in response to county‐level environmental enforcement is attenuated in counties exposed to increased bank regulatory oversight, as measured by severe enforcement actions. Furthermore, we show that the negative impact of bank regulatory oversight on small business lending to affected counties is the likely mechanism behind this on‐average effect. However, when bank regulatory oversight targets banks with stronger capitalization, we find that the negative effect of bank regulatory oversight on environmental enforcement is mitigated. Collectively, our findings suggest that bank regulation has a nuanced effect on the extent to which environmental enforcement reduces emissions, indicating that one regulator's mission can impact the objectives of another.

The effect of the mandatory disclosure of corporate tax returns on reporting bias

ERIC J. ALLEN, HENRY L. FRIEDMAN, AYDIN UYSAL
2026/10/11 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70087
Abstract

How do investor reactions to the mandatory disclosure of firm tax information influence managers' reporting choices? To answer this question, we build a structural model of reporting bias, which we test with a unique data set of Turkish firms that have been mandated to publicly disclose their tax returns since 2009. We find that both book and tax income numbers are informative to investors. Structural estimates are consistent with disclosure of the tax report reducing both market incentives to manage book earnings and investor uncertainty about underlying firm value. Tax disclosure is associated with higher reported tax income, though the increase is not statistically significant. Counterfactual estimates suggest that book‐tax‐conformity could change financial and tax reporting biases, but lead to a lower quality information environment relative to the separate disclosure of book and tax reports.

National Audit Oversight and Foreign Investment

James J. Blann, Phillip T. Lamoreaux, Beverly J. Larson
2026/11/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0320
Abstract

Public audit oversight bodies have become ubiquitous following the establishment of the U.S. PCAOB in 2002. However, despite their cost and prominence, little is known of their economic implications, if any. We examine the relation between establishment of public audit oversight bodies (POBs) and cross-border investment. Using a difference-in-differences design, we find that after a country establishes a POB, the country attracts more foreign capital. Our results suggest that total foreign portfolio investment (FPI) increases by approximately $11 billion for the average country that establishes a POB. The impact of POBs on foreign investment is dependent on the effectiveness of the POB in improving audit quality and the strength of a country’s existing institutional environment. Our study provides evidence of real economic effects of public audit oversight around the world, which adds to the literature on auditor oversight and to the broader literature on economic consequences of regulation. The Cost of Regulatory Inaction: Evidence from IFRS Nonadoption

Miao Liu, Wanrong Xu, Rachel Xi Zhang
2026/11/01 | 10.2308/tar-2023-0304
Abstract

Although extensive research examines the costs and benefits of adopting new disclosure regulations, little addresses the consequences of regulatory inaction. We investigate whether firms in nonadopting countries incur additional information losses, beyond the forgone benefits, when their foreign peers adopt International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). Nonadopters bear an absolute liquidity cost as foreign peers adopt IFRS, consistent with negative externalities from IFRS and accompanying reforms. Exploiting heterogeneity across nonadopting countries, we observe more pronounced liquidity declines among firms with greater institutional ownership overlap with IFRS peers. This effect appears more pronounced when IFRS peers are domiciled in countries with concurrent enforcement reforms or greater comparability improvements. Further evidence shows shared intermediaries—investors and analysts—reduce information production for nonadopters as other firms in their portfolios adopt IFRS. Overall, regulatory inaction degrades information environments: resources shift to the new regime, worsening liquidity for firms in the old regime. Data Availability: Data used in this study are available from commercial sources cited in the text. JEL Classifications: G1; K22; M41; M48.

Board–CEO Trust and Concealing Adverse News

Kee-Hong Bae, Sadok El Ghoul, Zhaoran Gong, Omrane Guedhami, Lixin Su
2026/11/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0298
Abstract

We investigate how generalized trust, a culturally rooted belief in the trustworthiness of unfamiliar others, influences board oversight. Using an ancestry-based measure of board–CEO trust based on ancestral origins and bilateral trust scores across countries, we find a significant positive relation between board–CEO trust and stock price crash risk, indicating higher bad-news hoarding. This relation remains robust across alternative crash risk measures, sample specifications, and trust measures. Firms with high board–CEO trust exhibit more asymmetric stock market reactions to good news versus bad news and a higher likelihood of material restatements. Analyses of insider trading patterns show that directors in high-trust firms are less informed than CEOs about adverse developments, supporting lax board monitoring rather than collusion between directors and CEOs. Overall, the evidence suggests that culturally inherited trust can weaken board oversight and reduce corporate transparency, consistent with an underexplored governance friction even within well-structured boards. Determinants and Consequences of Auditors’ Consultations with the National Office

Matthew G. Sherwood, Miguel Minutti-Meza, Aleksandra B. Zimmerman
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0348
Abstract

Audit teams may consult with their firm’s national office to obtain specialized expertise, satisfy regulatory and quality control requirements, and demonstrate due professional care. We describe the consultation process based on PCAOB standards, firm methodologies, and auditor interviews. We explain different types of consultations and highlight differences in the determinants and usefulness of mandatory versus voluntary consultations. Using PCAOB inspection data, we show that formal consultations are largely predictable based on engagement characteristics and, on average, are not associated with audit quality. However, two subsets of formal consultations that focus on accounting issues and occur earlier in the engagement process are associated with greater regulatory compliance and higher audit quality. Hence, an increasing reliance on all types of consultations does not automatically translate into higher audit quality. Collectively, our findings deepen our understanding of auditors’ use of national office consultations and their implications for audit practice. Beyond Greenwashing: Environmental Commitments and Gas Flaring in the African Oil Sector

Samuel Chang, Hans B. Christensen, Andrew McKinley
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2025-0361
Abstract

Corporations make substantial progress toward their Zero Routine Flaring commitments globally, particularly in Africa where preexisting environmental standards are weak. Moreover, committers’ flaring reductions on the continent are driven primarily by operational improvements rather than offloading assets and are concentrated among committers facing greater stakeholder pressure. Although flaring is higher for assets continuously operated by, awarded to, or divested to noncommitters, corporate actions to uphold commitments are associated with a conservatively estimated 10 percent net reduction in flaring in Africa without a corresponding reduction in oil production or local economic activity in extraction communities. Our findings suggest that environmental commitments are backed by corporate policies akin to establishing a uniform cost of pollution across the firm’s global operations and therefore may alleviate the problems posed by globally fragmented regulation. Internal Control over Financial Reporting and Mortgage Fraud

2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0214
Abstract

Mortgage fraud was a leading force behind the 2008 financial crisis. We measure the fraudulent income overstatement at the bank-ZIP Code-year level as the difference in the local growth between reported income in mortgage applications and the income based on IRS data. We find that overstated income is positively associated with banks’ internal control weaknesses over financial reporting and negatively associated with subsequent remediation of such weaknesses. How Black Accounting Faculty Exercise Agency to Navigate Career Advancement within Institutional Structures

Michelle K. Harding, Jennifer R. Joe, Kerri-Ann Sanderson
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0836
Abstract

This study examines how tenured BAF experience and navigate institutional structures while advancing their careers. Leveraging a qualitative approach, we find BAF encounter recurring forms of marginalization embedded in organizational structures that lead to social identity threats that destabilize their identities as scholars and teachers. BAF respond to these threats by employing agentic and self-preserving coping responses designed to affirm identity and negotiate career advancement. Although coping responses can be effective on an individual level, they do not transform institutional structures and can provoke pushback that reinscribes marginalization. We fill the void in the literature examining how career advancement unfolds within racialized conditions of academic institutions and advance research on minoritized group status in accounting academia.

Productivity versus Efficiency: The Effect of Incentive Focus on Target Setting in Participative Budgets

Jake Andrassy, Jason L. Brown, Timothy Mallon, Ashley K. Sauciuc
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2025-0493
Abstract

Firms typically design incentive systems to motivate employees and advance firm objectives, including productivity (i.e., maximizing output) and efficiency (i.e., minimizing input). However, there remains little empirical research on the potential psychological effects of adopting a productivity versus efficiency focus on employee risk perceptions and decision-making. To address this important gap, we examine how these different incentive foci affect employee target setting under participative budgets. We predict and find that employees perceive efficiency incentives as riskier than productivity incentives, leading to lower target setting. Moreover, consistent with our theory, this effect disappears in the absence of outcome uncertainty. We also find employees are less likely to achieve their target and have higher error rates under efficiency incentives, despite setting easier targets. Overall, our findings suggest that efficiency incentives, relative to productivity incentives, can have detrimental effects on target setting and subsequent performance in participative budgeting settings. Abstract

People’s right to free speech can have profound effects on how firms discharge their social and environmental responsibilities. We examine these effects by analyzing the impact of the staggered enactment of anti-SLAPP free speech laws across U.S. states on firms’ corporate social responsibility (CSR) behavior. Balancing Act: How Unaffiliated Analysts Navigate Private Information Disadvantages

Leila Peyravan, Brian Rountree, Konduru Sivaramakrishnan
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2025-0183
Abstract

Using analyst-lender affiliation in syndicated loans as a proxy for differential access to private information, we examine how unaffiliated analysts respond to this disadvantage. We find that unaffiliated analysts are more likely to herd toward affiliated analysts following loan origination, leading to improved forecast accuracy but weaker market reactions, consistent with reduced information content. In addition, herding analysts improve their forecast accuracy for other firms in their portfolios relative to nonherding analysts, suggesting a reallocation of effort. To capture information acquisition directly, we analyze IP address data from SEC EDGAR log files and find that unaffiliated analysts increase search activity, driven primarily by those not designated as herding. Although herding behavior wanes after the first year post-loan, elevated EDGAR searches persist for three years. Overall, our study provides novel evidence on how selective access to private information influences analysts’ competitive behavior. IPOs and Foreign Tax Structures

Christine L. Dobridge, Rebecca Lester, Andrew Whitten
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0048
Abstract

Does going public affect the amount and type of corporate international tax planning? Using a panel of U.S. corporate tax return data from 2004 to 2018, we show that IPO completion is associated with the rapid implementation of multinational foreign tax structures. Specifically, within three years after filing for an IPO, firms (1) expand their presence in low-tax jurisdictions, (2) enter into cross-border agreements that accompany intangible asset transfers to foreign subsidiaries, and (3) increase the likelihood and level of foreign related-party income-shifting payments. Effects are strongest among firms with strong capital market pressure to hit post-IPO earnings targets, firms with high R&D spending pre-IPO, and firms with limited ability to use net operating loss carryforwards. We contribute to the nascent literature studying tax implications of IPOs by documenting the types and timing of specific tax strategies that enable public firms to remain lightly taxed in the post-IPO period. Enabling Employee Adaptability to Change: The Joint Effect of Compensation Contract and Process Accountability

Marte Abts, Martine Cools, Sabra Khajehnejad, Alexandra Van den Abbeele
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0576
Abstract

Organizations increasingly operate in uncertain environments, requiring employees to revise task strategies to remain effective. We examine how pay for performance (PFP) influences adaptive performance following unanticipated change and argue that process accountability moderates this effect. Although PFP increases effort, it hinders adaptation by reinforcing persistence with an existing task strategy. Process accountability channels PFP-induced motivation into deeper strategic deliberation, enabling adaptation. We test our predictions in an experiment manipulating compensation (PFP versus flat wage) and process accountability (absent, reflection-only, and present), with participants in the reflection-only condition privately reflecting on their decision-making to isolate the social-evaluative component. Results show that PFP, relative to a flat wage, decreases adaptive performance when process accountability is absent, has no significant effect under reflection-only, and increases it when process accountability is present. Additional analyses suggest that strategic deliberation partially explains this effect. The findings underscore pairing PFP with process accountability to foster adaptation. The Signaling Role of Seemingly Myopic Investment Behavior

Cyrus Aghamolla, Vivian W. Fang, Renhui Fu
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0559
Abstract

This paper analytically and empirically investigates managerial investment responses to negative shocks in firm value. In the model, managers are privately informed about the shocks’ persistence, and those facing transitory shocks strategically underinvest to boost earnings and signal financial strength to investors. The model also predicts counter-signaling: firms with strong fundamentals do not need to cut investment to convey strength. We exploit the 2003 mutual fund trading scandal as a plausibly exogenous source of price pressure. Consistent with signaling, we find that firms with greater tainted-fund ownership are more likely to meet or narrowly beat analyst forecasts by cutting R&D, and that this behavior is associated with more favorable earnings announcement returns and better long-run performance. I explore the nature of analysts’ climate-related discussion in their reports. Audit Quality and Audit Partner Ownership of Non-Audit Businesses: Evidence from China

Clive S. Lennox, Zanyue Qi, Xi Wu
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2025-0720
Abstract

We examine whether audit partners’ ownership stakes in affiliated non-audit businesses are related to audit quality. We argue that non-audit ownership is a relatively sharp measure of auditor-client economic bonding, which can impair auditor independence. Using a unique setting in China where we observe partners’ equity stakes in both audit and non-audit businesses, we find that audit adjustments are significantly smaller when engagement partners hold larger ownership stakes in non-audit businesses. Our study informs ongoing policy debates on the structuring of audit and non-audit businesses as well as the investments of non-audit entities in accounting practices. Using Financial Accounting Information to Estimate the Income Shifting of US Multinationals

Scott D. Dyreng, Robert Hills, Kevin S. Markle
2026/11/06 | 10.1111/1911-3846.70099
Abstract

Existing estimates of how much income US multinational corporations (MNCs) shift out of the United States differ dramatically, and most cannot identify which specific firms do the shifting. We use data from firms' public financial reports to derive firm‐year estimates of US MNCs' outbound income shifting. We find that aggregate US outbound income shifting is substantially lower than widely cited macroeconomic‐based estimates, with the gap driven primarily by double counting of foreign income in macroeconomic data rather than by differences in firms' sensitivity to tax rates. Income shifting is also highly concentrated among a small number of firms, primarily in intangible‐intensive industries such as computers and pharmaceuticals, while most MNCs shift little or no income.
