Partisan Banks and Creditor Coordination Within Loan Syndicates

Robert M. Bushman, Sang Woo Sohn, Jianxin (Donny) Zhao
2026/11/16 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70097
Abstract

We investigate how firm‐level partisanship influences interactions between banks in the syndicated loan market. Because politically divergent banks hold conflicting perceptions of economic conditions, ideological distance between banks impedes consensus and elevates expected coordination costs. We find that greater political distance between a lead arranger and a participant reduces the participant's likelihood of joining a syndicate and lowers its retained loan share relative to the lead. The relation between political distance and loan share differences intensifies during periods of high national political polarization. Moreover, greater political diversity within a syndicate is associated with less restrictive loan covenants and higher interest spreads, reflecting a trade‐off where lenders sacrifice contingent control to avoid costly renegotiation gridlock. Validating our disagreement mechanism, we show that following presidential elections, banks politically misaligned with the incoming administration issue significantly more pessimistic macroeconomic forecasts than aligned banks.

The Role of Supply Chains in Climate Disclosure

Pietro Bonetti, Yang (Ellen) EN, Igor Kadach, Gaizka Ormazabal
2026/11/11 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70096
Abstract

We examine the role of supply chains in shaping the public disclosure of climate information. We leverage a unique empirical setting enabled by CDP—the leading platform for corporate climate risk reporting—which allows customers to demand climate‐related information from their suppliers. Using a large international sample of customer–supplier pairs, we find a positive association between customers’ information demand and suppliers’ public disclosure on the CDP. While the estimated association is modest on average, it exhibits substantial cross‐sectional variation reflecting the disclosure incentives of both customers and suppliers as well as their relative bargaining power. We also find descriptive evidence consistent with one potential implication of the documented association for corporate decision‐making: shifts in supply network relationships toward greener and more transparent suppliers. Overall, our findings contribute to ongoing debates on how to foster climate disclosure.

The Anti‐SLAPP Statutes and Corporate Environmental Policies

KEVIN C.W. CHEN, ZHIHONG CHEN, CHENGZHU SUN
2026/11/04 | 10.1111/1475-679x.70095
Abstract

We examine whether legal protection for civic participation affects corporate pollution. Polluters may deter public discipline through strategic lawsuits. Anti‐SLAPP (Strategic Lawsuit Against Public Participation) statutes mitigate this threat by enabling prompt dismissal and recovery of legal costs. Using a difference‐in‐differences design, we find that enacting an anti‐SLAPP statute in a state significantly reduces toxic emissions from that state by increasing local public pressure. The reduction is greater when the statutes provide stronger protection, when local stakeholders have stronger incentives and abilities to discipline polluters, and when local public pressure increases more after the enactment. Plants lower emissions by improving abatement rather than by reducing output or relocating emissions. Our paper underscores the role of legal protection in environmental governance and informs policy debates over anti‐SLAPP legislation.

National Audit Oversight and Foreign Investment

James J. Blann, Phillip T. Lamoreaux, Beverly J. Larson
2026/11/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0320
Abstract

Public audit oversight bodies have become ubiquitous following the establishment of the U.S. PCAOB in 2002. However, despite their cost and prominence, little is known of their economic implications, if any. We examine the relation between establishment of public audit oversight bodies (POBs) and cross-border investment. Using a difference-in-differences design, we find that after a country establishes a POB, the country attracts more foreign capital. Our results suggest that total foreign portfolio investment (FPI) increases by approximately $11 billion for the average country that establishes a POB. The impact of POBs on foreign investment is dependent on the effectiveness of the POB in improving audit quality and the strength of a country’s existing institutional environment. Our study provides evidence of real economic effects of public audit oversight around the world, which adds to the literature on auditor oversight and to the broader literature on economic consequences of regulation. The Cost of Regulatory Inaction: Evidence from IFRS Nonadoption

Miao Liu, Wanrong Xu, Rachel Xi Zhang
2026/11/01 | 10.2308/tar-2023-0304
Abstract

Although extensive research examines the costs and benefits of adopting new disclosure regulations, little addresses the consequences of regulatory inaction. We investigate whether firms in nonadopting countries incur additional information losses, beyond the forgone benefits, when their foreign peers adopt International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). Nonadopters bear an absolute liquidity cost as foreign peers adopt IFRS, consistent with negative externalities from IFRS and accompanying reforms. Exploiting heterogeneity across nonadopting countries, we observe more pronounced liquidity declines among firms with greater institutional ownership overlap with IFRS peers. This effect appears more pronounced when IFRS peers are domiciled in countries with concurrent enforcement reforms or greater comparability improvements. Further evidence shows shared intermediaries—investors and analysts—reduce information production for nonadopters as other firms in their portfolios adopt IFRS. Overall, regulatory inaction degrades information environments: resources shift to the new regime, worsening liquidity for firms in the old regime. Data Availability: Data used in this study are available from commercial sources cited in the text. JEL Classifications: G1; K22; M41; M48.

Board–CEO Trust and Concealing Adverse News

Kee-Hong Bae, Sadok El Ghoul, Zhaoran Gong, Omrane Guedhami, Lixin Su
2026/11/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0298
Abstract

We investigate how generalized trust, a culturally rooted belief in the trustworthiness of unfamiliar others, influences board oversight. Using an ancestry-based measure of board–CEO trust based on ancestral origins and bilateral trust scores across countries, we find a significant positive relation between board–CEO trust and stock price crash risk, indicating higher bad-news hoarding. This relation remains robust across alternative crash risk measures, sample specifications, and trust measures. Firms with high board–CEO trust exhibit more asymmetric stock market reactions to good news versus bad news and a higher likelihood of material restatements. Analyses of insider trading patterns show that directors in high-trust firms are less informed than CEOs about adverse developments, supporting lax board monitoring rather than collusion between directors and CEOs. Overall, the evidence suggests that culturally inherited trust can weaken board oversight and reduce corporate transparency, consistent with an underexplored governance friction even within well-structured boards. Private Firm Disclosures and Public Firms' Debt Choice

Fengqin Chen, Jeffrey Pittman, Walid Saffar
2026/11/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0646
Abstract

Analyzing a cross-country sample, we provide evidence implying that private firm disclosure transparency narrows information asymmetry, which, in turn, lowers public firms' reliance on bank debt. Channel tests help substantiate that private firm disclosure elicits this positive externality, evident in lower monitoring demand, lower borrowing costs, and higher credit rating quality. Cross-sectional results suggest that the impact of private firm transparency is larger when public firms suffer worse information opacity, private firm disclosures are more important to their public peers, and bond markets are more developed. In relying on the staggered implementation of electronic business registers as a plausibly exogenous shock and a simulated disclosure scope as the instrumental variable, we continue to find that private firm disclosures reduce public firms' bank debt reliance, helping to alleviate endogeneity threats. Target Firm Material Contract Redactions and Valuation in Acquisitions

Paul A. Griffin, Hyun A. Hong, Kyungran Lee, Jiwoo Ryou
2026/11/01 | 10.2308/tar-2023-0169
Abstract

We study whether and how the SEC’s confidential treatment program, which allows firms to redact proprietary information from material contracts, affects valuation in corporate acquisitions. We find that friendly acquisitions of redacting targets generate significantly higher positive announcement returns for both targets and acquirers compared with acquisitions of nonredacting targets. These positive returns are also more pronounced when targets are likelier to hold valuable proprietary information. In addition, friendly acquisitions of redacting targets achieve better long-term performance and recognize fewer subsequent goodwill impairments compared with acquisitions of nonredacting targets. Our findings of a robust association between target firm material contract redactions and target and acquirer announcement returns are consistent with a dual disclosure perspective, whereby redaction preserves target proprietary value, which is transferred privately to the acquirer through due diligence. The Effect of Cost Elasticity Choice on Loss Reversal

Edmond Tapero, Dan Weiss
2026/11/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0581
Abstract

Although the prevalence of losses has been increasing over the years, little is known about how managerial decisions lead to reversing losses back to profits. We find cost elasticity choices, implied by operating actions made in advance, significantly increase the likelihood of loss reversal. We show loss firms with high cost elasticity exhibit significantly higher long-term survival rates and are therefore less likely to exercise the abandonment option than loss firms with low cost elasticity. The contributions are twofold. First, the findings shed light on a mechanism underlying loss reversal. Rational Information Acquisition Screens

Paul E. Fischer, Mirko S. Heinle, Jung Min Kim, Christina Zhu
2026/11/01 | 10.2308/tar-2024-0799
Abstract

We develop and empirically test a model of information acquisition in capital markets. In the model, investors are uncertain about the returns to acquiring private information before they acquire it. This results in a novel reason for why past price movements affect future capital market outcomes: investors use public information, including past prices, as a screen to estimate the returns to acquiring private information and efficiently allocate their limited information-processing capacity across firms. The model predicts that larger unexplained price movements lead to more private information acquisition, higher future price volatility, and higher future expected trading volume. Using fine-grained data measuring information acquisition on EDGAR and Bloomberg, we provide empirical evidence in support of the model’s predictions. I explore the nature of analysts’ climate-related discussion in their reports. Audit Quality and Audit Partner Ownership of Non-Audit Businesses: Evidence from China

Clive S. Lennox, Zanyue Qi, Xi Wu
2026/10/01 | 10.2308/tar-2025-0720
Abstract

We examine whether audit partners’ ownership stakes in affiliated non-audit businesses are related to audit quality. We argue that non-audit ownership is a relatively sharp measure of auditor-client economic bonding, which can impair auditor independence. Using a unique setting in China where we observe partners’ equity stakes in both audit and non-audit businesses, we find that audit adjustments are significantly smaller when engagement partners hold larger ownership stakes in non-audit businesses. Our study informs ongoing policy debates on the structuring of audit and non-audit businesses as well as the investments of non-audit entities in accounting practices. I examine how the use of sales performance measures in CEO incentive contracts affects the operations of a firm and its suppliers. The Impact of Mandatory Audit Firm Rotation on Competitive Pricing Strategies for New Audit Engagements: Evidence From the European Union

Ditmir Sufaj, Simon Dekeyser, Marleen Willekens
2026/11/18 | 10.1111/1911-3846.70102
Abstract

Mandatory audit firm rotation was introduced in the European Union (EU), in part, to make the audit market more dynamic by bringing audit engagements to market more frequently. However, it creates a countervailing economic force: By limiting the duration of auditor–client relationships, mandatory rotation shortens the period over which auditors can recoup first‐year fee discounts through fees earned in subsequent years. Consistent with this argument, we find that first‐year fee discounts decline substantially after mandatory firm rotation took effect in the EU, particularly in countries with stricter limits on auditor tenure. Thus, greater opportunities to compete for new engagements do not necessarily translate into greater first‐year fee discounts. Our findings highlight that regulators should consider not only how frequently audit engagements are brought to market but also how limits on auditor tenure shape the pricing incentives associated with competing for new clients.

Intertemporal Dynamics of Cost Asymmetry

Eti Einhorn, Efrat Shust
2026/11/11 | 10.1111/1911-3846.70097
Abstract

We study the intertemporal dynamics of asymmetric cost behavior in a competitive setting. Our analysis delves into the primitives of cost asymmetry and explores its dynamic evolution over time. The analysis yields the result that cost behavior is asymmetric by default and path‐dependent, transpiring as cost stickiness following prior resource expansion and taking the form of cost anti‐stickiness after previous resource contraction. The analysis further suggests that the magnitude of cost asymmetry, either stickiness or anti‐stickiness, is decreasing in demand uncertainty and competition intensity, increasing in adjustment costs and time discounting, and greater for small demand shocks than for large demand shocks. By highlighting cost asymmetry as a dynamic, rather than static, phenomenon, and by providing new, testable predictions regarding the direction and magnitude of cost asymmetry across firms and over time, our results offer theoretical grounding for the empirical cost asymmetry literature and may also help investors and other stakeholders interpret cost behavior in light of firms' recent history of expansion or contraction and their competitive environment.

The Effect of Non‐US Tax Authority Monitoring on Income Shifting by Affiliates of US Multinationals: Evidence From EDGAR Search Activity

Sabrina Chi, Anh V. Persson, Terry Shevlin, Oktay Urcan
2026/11/10 | 10.1111/1911-3846.70101
Abstract

Governments worldwide are concerned that multinational enterprises (MNEs) shift profits across borders to avoid taxes. While prior research shows that home‐country tax enforcement can reduce tax avoidance, far less is known about whether foreign tax authorities (FTAs) can constrain income shifting by MNE affiliates. We measure FTA monitoring using data on FTAs' downloads of US MNEs' annual reports filed with the SEC. We find that FTA monitoring is more intensive in higher‐tax‐rate countries with a larger MNE presence, consistent with those jurisdictions facing stronger incentives to monitor income shifting. More importantly, we find that monitoring by an affiliate's local tax authority does not systematically reduce income shifting by US MNE affiliates, whereas aggregate monitoring in the MNE group affects an affiliate even when its own local FTA is not observably monitoring it. This result is consistent with a spillover effect of FTA monitoring. This association is stronger when FTAs operate under information‐exchange treaties, when company disclosures are more detailed, and when enforcement capacity is greater. We also find positive associations between aggregate FTA monitoring and subsequent tax avoidance and tax settlements of US MNEs. Our findings suggest monitoring by multiple FTAs effectively combats MNE tax avoidance and have implications for how tax administrations design and coordinate monitoring efforts.

Using Financial Accounting Information to Estimate the Income Shifting of US Multinationals

Scott D. Dyreng, Robert Hills, Kevin S. Markle
2026/11/06 | 10.1111/1911-3846.70099
Abstract

Existing estimates of how much income US multinational corporations (MNCs) shift out of the United States differ dramatically, and most cannot identify which specific firms do the shifting. We use data from firms' public financial reports to derive firm‐year estimates of US MNCs' outbound income shifting. We find that aggregate US outbound income shifting is substantially lower than widely cited macroeconomic‐based estimates, with the gap driven primarily by double counting of foreign income in macroeconomic data rather than by differences in firms' sensitivity to tax rates. Income shifting is also highly concentrated among a small number of firms, primarily in intangible‐intensive industries such as computers and pharmaceuticals, while most MNCs shift little or no income. This concentration implies that size‐based thresholds embedded in the OECD's Pillar 2 and the US corporate alternative minimum tax will capture the most active income shifters but may also impose compliance burdens on many firms that shift minimal income. These findings have direct implications for policy‐makers designing international tax rules, tax authorities prioritizing audit resources toward high‐risk income shifters, and investors and lenders assessing MNCs' tax‐related exposures.

Mandatory Disclosure and Peer Firms' Learning From Stock Prices

Jaewoo Kim, Hunter Pearson, Brady Twedt
2026/10/30 | 10.1111/1911-3846.70094
Abstract

Theory posits that mandatory disclosure influences peer firms' learning from stock prices in two non‐mutually exclusive ways: intra‐industry information transfers and the reallocation of information acquisition efforts. Using mandatory segment disclosure as our setting, we document a decrease in investment‐ q sensitivity for peers of disclosing firms. Whose Job Is It, Anyway? The Effect of the Review Process on Employee Performance

Adam Bross, Ryan D. Sommerfeldt
2026/10/29 | 10.1111/1911-3846.70095
Abstract

Companies have widely adopted a system of work‐product reviews whereby the work of one individual is subsequently reviewed by another to ensure its quality. We examine how managers can effectively implement a review of employee work to improve performance. Organizations review employees' work to identify mistakes and improve work quality. However, reviews may also reduce employees' effort and initiative if they expect reviewers to identify and correct their errors for them. We use an experiment to examine how employee performance depends on who conducts the review and how feedback is provided. Specifically, participants' work was reviewed by either a superior or a peer, and the reviewer provided either general, high‐level guidance (autonomy‐oriented) or specific corrective information about errors (dependency‐oriented). Employees performed best when superiors provided high‐level guidance or when peers provided specific corrective feedback, with these benefits emerging over time. Guidance from a superior increased employees' sense of responsibility, whereas corrective help from a peer strengthened feelings of teamwork and cohesion. Review approaches that did not fit these combinations offered no clear performance benefit relative to having no review. The findings show that review effectiveness depends on matching the feedback approach to the reviewer's organizational rank. These results have important implications for managers, accountants responsible for internal controls, and audit professionals involved in designing and implementing the review process.

XBRL adoption and SEC monitoring

Minjae Koo, Yinqi Zhang, Meiling Zhao, Yuping Zhao
2026/11/26 | 10.1007/s11142-026-10003-2
Abstract

We examine how disclosure processing costs influence SEC monitoring of firm disclosures by leveraging the mandatory adoption of XBRL—a digital reporting language designed to reduce these costs. Post adoption, both the frequency and quality of SEC comment letters for 10-K filings increase. Additional tests suggest that XBRL enhances SEC monitoring through three channels. Overall, our evidence suggests that reduced disclosure processing costs enhance the SEC’s regulatory monitoring.

Disclosing work-from-home flexibility to compete for talent? Evidence from job postings

Charles Ham, Rebecca N. Hann, Wenfeng Wang, Jingwen Yang
2026/11/21 | 10.1007/s11142-026-10001-4
Abstract

Job postings serve as one of the primary means to communicate with prospective employees, enabling employers to highlight the benefits of the position and attract qualified applicants. As work-from-home flexibility has become increasingly prevalent, this study examines whether employers strategically disclose work-from-home flexibility in their job postings and how this disclosure is associated with hiring outcomes. Using a comprehensive sample of job postings from US employers between 2017 and 2024, we find that employers are more likely to disclose work-from-home flexibility when facing greater labor market competition and that disclosing this flexibility is associated with a higher job vacancy filling rate, especially when work-from-home information is disclosed in a more salient manner.
